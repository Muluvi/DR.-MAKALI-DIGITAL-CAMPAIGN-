# Digital cannot win this election

This is the constraint the entire strategy is built around, and it is worth stating before any
platform, budget or content calendar is discussed.

According to the 2019 census, **13.6% of Kitui's population aged three and above uses the
internet** — 143,340 people out of 1,053,991. The remaining **86.4% live in an offline media
environment.**

Applied to the register, the connected minority is roughly **72,000 reachable voters.**

If the campaign captured every single internet-connected voter in Kitui County — all of them,
at 100%, which no campaign in history has done — it would assemble about 72,000 voters against
a threshold of 198,004. And reach is not votes. The ceiling is roughly 36% of what winning
requires.

Digital cannot deliver this election. It can shape it.

## What digital does well

**It sets the agenda for radio.** Facebook and X dominate the information diet of local radio
producers, vernacular talk-show hosts, church leaders and county assembly members. Winning the
rapid-response contest on those platforms shapes what is broadcast six hours later to an
audience twenty times larger.

**It reaches the diaspora.** More than 150,000 Kamba live outside the county, concentrated in
Nairobi and the Coast. Geofenced advertising converts them into donors and, more importantly,
into family opinion-shapers whose endorsements carry weight in rural households.

**It recruits.** The youth cohort — roughly 234,000 registered voters — supplies the field
activists, ground logistics and polling agents the campaign runs on.

**It tests cheaply.** Hyper-geofencing Kitui CBD and Mwingi Central allows policy messages to
be tested at low cost before they are committed to radio and print.

## What digital cannot do

It cannot deliver 200,000 votes, being capped at roughly 72,000 reachable voters in-county. It
cannot penetrate the rural agrarian base, where smartphone ownership is low, data costs are
high and 3G coverage is weak across Tharaka, Tseikuru, Voo/Kyamatu, Endau and Mutha. And it
cannot counteract ground rumour directly — WhatsApp and Facebook echo chambers rarely cross
into dryland farming communities without a physical intermediary.

## The infrastructure that actually reaches people

The election is won or lost across roughly 460,000 offline voters. Six channels reach them, and
their capacities are known.

**Kikamba vernacular radio** reaches approximately 420,000 voters — 78.8% of the register — at
78 to 80% daily and weekly listenership. It is the decisive medium. Musyi FM, County FM and
Wikwatyo FM carry the placement budget; morning studio interviews between 06:30 and 08:30,
45-second Kikamba testimonials from real farmers in Mwingi North, Kitui South and Kitui Rural,
and live outside broadcasts from market rallies at Kalundu, Kabati and Mutomo.

**Direct 2G SMS** reaches approximately 320,000 voters, 60.1% of the register, on basic GSM
handsets — hyper-localised Kikamba messages timed to market-day eves and election morning.

**A zero-rated USSD service** reaches approximately 250,000 feature-phone voters, 46.9% of the
register: the manifesto in Kikamba, volunteer registration and ward captain lookup, dialled
free.

**Open-air market barazas** reach approximately 280,000 voters, 52.6% of the register, on a
fixed weekly rotation — Nguni and Kabati on Monday, Kalundu and Tseikuru on Tuesday, Mbondoni
and Mutomo on Wednesday, Chuluni and Kyuso on Thursday, Kisasi and Migwani on Friday, Kitui and
Mwingi town markets on Saturday.

**Church and faith networks** reach approximately 350,000 weekly attendees, 65.7% of the
register, across Catholic, AIC, Redeemed Gospel, Anglican and Baptist congregations.

**Mobile-money agents** reach approximately 180,000 voters monthly, 33.8% of the register.
These are regulated financial infrastructure: any commercial arrangement with agents must be
reviewed by campaign counsel before recruitment begins, and the kiosk and ambassador counts
still need a source.

## Where the weight goes

The common failure of modern Kenyan campaigns is over-weighting visible social media while
starving the unglamorous rural infrastructure that actually produces votes. What follows is a
rebalancing target — the share of communications *effort* each domain carries in production
time, team attention and weekly output. It is where the campaign's weight goes, not what
anything costs.

| Domain | Conventional pitch | Rebalanced | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Digital and social | 45% | **18%** | Right-sized to 13.6% in-county penetration plus diaspora |
| Vernacular radio | 20% | **37%** | Scaled to dominate the medium reaching 78%+ of voters daily |
| 2G SMS and USSD | 10% | **20%** | Doubled to build a direct pipeline to 320,000 feature-phone voters |
| Market caravans and barazas | 15% | **18%** | Physical presence and live momentum on market days |
| Church and community | 10% | **7%** | Focused, protocol-compliant clergy partnership |

Eighty-two per cent offline, eighteen per cent digital. From a digital consultancy, that is the
recommendation the evidence supports, and making it is the point.

## Who controls the air

One complication sits on top of the radio strategy. The highest-reach Kikamba stations are
owned, financed or editorially steered by active political rivals and party gatekeepers.

Athiani FM is associated with Wiper party leader Kalonzo Musyoka — party-sensitive rather than
hostile, and most consequential during the nomination window, when coverage may track party
leadership sentiment rather than merit. Mbaitu FM and Syokimau FM carry rival associations and
are monitoring targets, not placement targets. The stations open to the campaign are the
commercially or institutionally independent ones: Musyi FM, County FM and Wikwatyo FM.

Three structural hazards follow. Editorial blackouts, where clean audit certificates and
parliamentary budget wins are denied airtime on captured stations. Studio ambushes, where
morning panels between 06:00 and 09:00 are stacked with rival proxies to draw the candidate
into unverified local controversy. And arbitrary rate inflation, where politically steered
houses impose surcharges or refuse prime slots outright.

The answer is a bypass architecture that does not depend on any station's goodwill: neutral and
church-owned stations, the zero-rated USSD gateway, sound trucks across all eight
sub-counties, and pre-loaded audio distributed through megaphones and WhatsApp voice notes at
baraza level.

Ownership associations are publicly reported rather than certified, and media ownership
changes. The map is verified and updated before any placement is booked.
